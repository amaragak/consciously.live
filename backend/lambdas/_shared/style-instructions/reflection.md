> Guidance for Reflection meditations, not fixed rules. Use judgement for each moment; the user's request overrides any of this.

# Writing a Reflection meditation

## Mechanism
Reflection turns attention inward to answer, not just to observe. Settle the listener, hand them one clear prompt, then leave real silence for them to answer privately. The felt result comes from the listener's own material (a memory, a value, a choice), which you only invite.
- Ask less, and leave more room. One question held well works better than five stacked.
- Anchor each answer in the body ("where do you feel that?"). This turns a thought into a felt shift.
- Warmth and permission make honest answers possible. Never ask the listener to defend an answer.

## Phases
Build the plan from these phases. Combine or drop them to fit length and request.
1. **Settle** (about 10–15%): posture, breaths, body arrival, permission to do nothing.
2. **Frame** (5–10%): name the theme or intention in plain words. Say why this reflection is safe to do.
3. **Core reflection** (35–50%): the prompts. Each prompt gets its own beat: invite, sense, wait.
4. **Embodiment or offering** (15–25%): hand on heart, warmth or light, and phrases to repeat (gratitude, forgiveness, kind wishes).
5. **Rest** (10%): drop the technique and sit with what's here.
6. **Return** (5–10%): a deepening breath, attention back to the room, a carry-forward line.

A change of technique (asking → imagining → repeating → resting) marks a phase boundary. Longer sessions add more prompts or a second cycle (for example a second person or situation) rather than stretching each beat. Carry one thread across all phases: the user's theme, a recurring image, or one phrase that returns, varied slightly each time. End each section on a line the next one can pick up ("hold that feeling as we turn to…").

Default arc: settle → frame → question or memory → feel it in the body → offer a phrase or gesture → rest → return.

## How to say it
- **Cue anatomy:** name the focus, invite softly, leave the answer open. Example: "Bring to mind a moment this week you felt thankful. Let whatever appears first be the right one."
- Use "you" and present tense. Add a few first-person lines the listener can repeat silently ("I forgive myself", "may I be at ease"). Introduce them with "if it feels right, say silently…".
- Questions: short, concrete, one at a time. Move from easier to deeper ("what are you grateful for?" → "what does this part of you need?"). Offer a way in for those who draw a blank ("if no words come, notice sensations").
- Soften with "you might", "perhaps", "if you like". Add "nothing to fix" language where the topic is tender.
- Use plain, sensory words: warmth, weight, tightness, softening. Short sentences. Repeat key lines once or twice for rhythm and vary the wording.
- Weave the user's material in by naming it in the prompts. Reframe a struggle as the question itself ("what is this asking of you?") rather than quoting the brief back at them.
- For difficult material, invite something manageable rather than the worst moment, and offer a way out ("if it feels too much, return to your breath").

## Pauses
A pause gives the listener time to do what the last line asked, so its length follows the task. Breath and body cues take about 2–4s. A real question or memory prompt takes 10–30s or more. Spoken phrases to repeat take about 5–8s. Silence grows as the session deepens, and the longest rest comes near the end. Don't pause mid-sentence, or right after a question that needs a longer answer. Write as [[PAUSE Ns]].

## Opening and closing
Open with a brief welcome and posture, settling breaths, body arrival, and a stated intention. Add permission ("nothing to fix") when the topic is tender. Close by deepening once more, then return attention to the room and body. Add a short gratitude line, then a carry-forward line, then eyes open. Adapt both to the request (a morning session carries into the day, a night session lets go into rest).

## What listeners expect
- Most sessions open with posture, then settling breaths and body arrival. Many start with a welcome, and most state an intention. Many close with a deepening breath, a return, gratitude, and a carry-forward line.
- Permissive, soothing, warm. Mostly "you", present tense, with some first-person lines. Moderate questions. Invitations to repeat phrases are common.
- Fitting phrasing: kind wishes ("may I be at ease"), gratitude and self-forgiveness, remembering inner truth, felt warmth or energy, and "nothing to fix".
- Keep rare: generic relax-and-release, body-part-locating, and "notice how you feel" lines.
- Avoid: power or strength affirmations (affirmation-loop), formal safety or happiness wishes for others (loving-kindness), calm-state visualisation scripts (visualization), and arm-placement instructions (movement).

## Common mistakes
- Stacking questions with no silence. Pose one, then wait.
- Explaining or lecturing mid-practice. Keep teaching to a sentence or two.
- Forcing positive answers. Allow mixed feelings.
- Turning the session into a pure affirmation list. Questions come first and phrases follow.
- Ignoring the user's theme in the middle. Name it.

## Worked example
**Request:** "10 minutes, reflecting on whether I'm living by my values after a stressful year."
- **Settle:** arrive, breathe, permission to be honest without judging.
- **Frame:** one value as the lens ("what matters most right now?"). The thread is "what matters".
- **Core reflection:** a recent moment you acted in line with it, then a moment you drifted. Feel each in the body.
- **Offering:** one gentle choice for tomorrow, plus a silent phrase of self-kindness.
- **Return:** deepen, thank yourself, carry the value into the day.
